A third group, voice-cloning and stem tools, overlaps heavily with the AI voice space covered in our [ElevenLabs review](https://www.aitoolcrux.com/blog/elevenlabs-review-2026/).

The practical difference shows up in three dimensions:

|---|---|---|---|
| Primary output | Full songs with vocals | Full songs with vocals | Instrumental beats and loops |

None of the three is universally "best." The decision matrix is short: if your bottleneck is finishing a song idea, use a prompt-to-song tool; if your bottleneck is legal clearance for a video, use a copyright-safe beat maker.

## How do you write a good prompt for AI music?

Prompt quality is the difference between "AI-sounding pop" and a track you would actually release. The prompt-to-song models respond strongly to structure, so the practical formula is: genre, tempo, mood, vocal style, and a lyrical or instrumental reference. "A wistful bluegrass song about being ghosted on a dating app, solo male vocal, acoustic guitar, 100 BPM" produces far more consistent results than "make me a sad song" — the same way the example prompts on Udio's homepage work ("Love song about bread crumbs from a pigeon's perspective, 1950s, doo wop" is specific enough for the model to anchor genre conventions).

Three prompt habits matter most:

- **Anchor the genre first.** Genre tags change everything: the same lyric prompt produces a different song in doo wop vs. gothic country vs. R&B.
- **Specify the vocal and instrumentation.** Solo vocal, duet, male/female, acoustic vs. electronic — these are the levers that separate generic output from a track with a point of view.
- **Iterate on sections, not whole songs.** Re-roll the verse, extend the bridge, or change the key rather than regenerating from scratch; this is where credit consumption hides, and the "credits burn fast" complaint from video tools applies identically here.

The counter-intuitive finding from testing: short prompts produce more generic output than long ones. The models have seen the short-form average thousands of times; they reproduce it. Specificity is what pushes a generation past the median.

## What are the licensing and copyright risks with AI music?

The legal risks divide into three buckets, and they are not the same risk. First, **model training risk**: was the model trained on copyrighted music without a license? This is the legal question behind every "copyright-safe" marketing claim. Soundraw's answer is the cleanest of the three — the homepage states its AI is "trained only on music we create in-house," so the training-data question is answered by design. Suno and Udio do not make that claim, which means the training question remains an open legal question for their outputs, even though paid-tier ownership is clearly granted in their terms.

Second, **output ownership risk**: who owns the generated track? All three grant you ownership or commercial rights on paid tiers — free tiers are where rights are most restricted, so publishing free-tier output commercially is where users get burned.

Third, **voice and likeness risk**: if you clone a real singer's voice or generate a track that closely mimics a copyrighted style, you can still face takedowns regardless of what the tool's terms say. The tool's license covers the generation; it does not immunize you against a style or voice infringement claim.

The practical rule: for monetized content, prefer Soundraw's in-house-trained model when you need beats, and keep purchase records and prompt logs for any Suno or Udio track you release commercially.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is AI-generated music copyright-free in 2026?

No — "copyright-free" and "licensed for commercial use" are different things. Suno and Udio give you ownership of songs you create on paid plans, which means you hold rights but must keep records of the generations. Soundraw's model is trained in-house and every beat is "legally yours to use, monetize, and share," which is the closest thing to worry-free commercial use in the category.

### Can I use AI music on YouTube or monetized videos?

Yes, on every one of these tools, but the terms differ. On Soundraw, commercial use is the core promise of the product. On Suno and Udio, ownership and commercial use come with paid plans — free-tier generations generally do not carry the same commercial rights. If you monetize content, read the plan terms before publishing, not after.

### Does Soundraw work like Suno?

Different tools, different jobs. Suno and Udio output complete songs — vocals, instruments, and mastering — from a text prompt. Soundraw outputs instrumental beats and loops chosen by mood, genre, and tempo. One finishes your song idea; the other clears your background music.

### What is the best free AI music generator in 2026?

Suno's free tier (daily credits) is the most usable sustained free option for prompt-to-song generation. Udio's free trial is best for evaluating genre fidelity before paying. Soundraw's free trial is the right entry point if your goal is copyright-safe background music.

### How much does Suno cost per month?

Suno uses a freemium model: a free tier with daily credits plus paid Pro plans for more credits, faster generation, and commercial ownership. Prices change, so verify the current tiers on the official pricing page before subscribing.
